Physics & Research Notes

What the chart actually is

A Smith chart is a polar plot of the complex voltage reflection coefficient Γ, mapped so that lines of constant normalised resistance and constant normalised reactance become circles and arcs. It replaces messy complex-number arithmetic for impedance matching and transmission-line problems with a purely graphical (or in this tool, live-computed) procedure.

Reflection coefficient

Γ = (Z − Z₀) / (Z + Z₀)

Z is the load (or local line) impedance, Z₀ is the system's characteristic impedance. Γ is a complex number with |Γ| ≤ 1 for any passive load; |Γ| = 0 means a perfect match (all power absorbed, no reflection), |Γ| = 1 means total reflection (open or short circuit, or any purely reactive load).

The chart mapping (why circles and arcs appear)

z = Z/Z₀ = (1 + Γ) / (1 − Γ)

Writing z = r + jx (normalised resistance/reactance) and Γ = u + jv, and substituting, the condition "r = constant" algebraically becomes a circle in (u,v): centre (r/(1+r), 0), radius 1/(1+r). The condition "x = constant" becomes an arc of a circle: centre (1, 1/x), radius 1/x, always intersecting the point (1,0). That's the entire chart — it's just Cartesian resistance/reactance grid lines conformally mapped into the unit disc.

VSWR and return loss

VSWR = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|)   ·   RL(dB) = −20 log₁₀|Γ|

Concentric circles centred on the chart's origin are circles of constant |Γ|, which means constant VSWR — this is exactly the circle a lossless transmission line's impedance traces out as you move along it (Tab 2). A matched load sits at the centre (VSWR = 1, RL = ∞ dB); the outer rim is VSWR = ∞ (0 dB return loss, total reflection).

Admittance and the 180° rotation trick

y = Y/Y₀ = 1/z   ·   Γᶜ = −Γ᷋

Because 1/z corresponds to reflecting Γ through the origin, the exact same chart can be read as an admittance chart simply by rotating any impedance point 180° (or equivalently, reading the "impedance" grid upside-down). This is why the classic paper Smith chart often has both an impedance grid and its mirror-image admittance grid printed together — extremely useful for stub-matching designs that alternate between series (impedance-friendly) and shunt (admittance-friendly) elements.

Moving along a transmission line

Γ(l) = Γᵟ · e−j2βl   ,   β = 2π/λ

For a lossless line of characteristic impedance Z₀, the reflection coefficient at a distance l back from the load has the same magnitude as Γᵟ (the wave doesn't attenuate) but a phase that rotates linearly with distance — because the reflected wave has to travel the extra distance l twice (out to the load's reference plane and back). One full revolution around the chart therefore corresponds to l = λ/2, not a full wavelength. Voltage maxima on the line occur exactly where the chart point crosses the positive real axis (Γ purely real and positive); voltage minima occur at the negative real axis.

The printed rim: angle and wavelength scales

A professional paper Smith chart surrounds the unit circle with two extra rings you won't find on a bare impedance grid: an angle-of-Γ scale in degrees, and a pair of wavelength scales — "wavelengths toward generator" (WTG) and "wavelengths toward load" (WTL) — both running 0 to 0.5λ from the same zero point (the leftmost, short-circuit end of the real axis) but in opposite directions. This tool draws that same rim (toggle it off if you want a cleaner grid). A handy self-check when reading any real chart: at any point, WTG + WTL = 0.5 always — if your two readings don't add up, you've misread a scale.

Reading the halves of the chart

The upper half of the chart (v > 0) is where x > 0 — inductive reactance. The lower half (v < 0) is where x < 0 — capacitive reactance. The real axis itself (v = 0) is purely resistive. Three landmark points worth memorising: the centre (1,0 in normalised z, i.e. Z = Z₀) is a perfect match; the rightmost point (r = ∞) is an open circuit; the leftmost point (r = 0) is a short circuit.

Scope & simplifications

This tool assumes lossless transmission lines (no attenuation constant α) for the rotation model in Tab 2, which is the standard textbook Smith-chart assumption and matches how the chart is used for matching-network design in practice. Real lines have some loss, which causes the |Γ| circle to spiral very slightly inward as you move toward the generator rather than staying perfectly circular — a second-order effect not modelled here. For production RF matching work, cross-check final designs against a full linear circuit simulator (e.g. an S-parameter/ABCD-matrix tool) that accounts for dielectric and conductor losses, dispersion, and component parasitics.
