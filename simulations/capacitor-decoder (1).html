C1

The 3-Digit / 4-Digit Code Reader

Used on ceramic disc, multilayer ceramic (MLCC), and most box-style film capacitors (PET / MPB / MKT / MKP). The first two digits are significant figures, the third is a multiplier — the result is always in picofarads (pF).

Rule of thumb: a lone 2-digit number under 100 with no third digit (like "47") is usually already in pF. A code with a letter "R" in the middle (like "4R7") means "decimal point here" — no multiplier at all, so 4R7 = 4.7 pF.
C2

Tolerance & Voltage Codes

The letters riding after the value tell you how much the real capacitance can drift, and the two-character codes tell you the maximum working voltage — both defined by the EIA (Electronic Industries Alliance).

Tolerance letters

How far the actual value may stray from what's printed.

Voltage codes (DC)

A digit + a letter, e.g. 2A = 100 VDC. Often tacked onto the end of a value, e.g. 103J2A = 10 nF, ±5%, 100 V.

Heads up: not every manufacturer follows the EIA table strictly — some print the working voltage directly instead (like plain "250V"), and some SMD tantalum parts reuse a single voltage letter with the leading digit dropped. When in doubt, the datasheet wins.
C3

SMD & Chip Capacitor Codes

Surface-mount parts are often too small for a 3-digit print, so many use a 2-character shorthand: two digits for the significant figures, one letter for the multiplier.

SMD tantalum voltage letters

Chip tantalum capacitors are polarized and often mark voltage with a single letter instead of the tolerance letter — this sits next to a directly-printed µF value, e.g. 106 + C = 10 µF, 16 V.

Reading Electrolytics & Direct-Print Capacitors

Aluminium electrolytics and larger film/motor capacitors are usually roomy enough to skip the code entirely — they print the real value straight on the case. The catch is polarity: get it backwards and the capacitor can fail, bulge, or vent.

1
Capacitance, written straight up"470µF" means 470 microfarads — no multiplier math needed. If you see a bare number like "220" with no unit on a can, it's almost always µF too.
2
Working voltageThe max DC voltage it can safely hold, e.g. "25V". Always replace with equal or higher voltage — never lower.
3
The stripe marks the negative leadOn radial (both-leads-down) electrolytics, a dark stripe with a minus sign runs down the negative side. On axial cans, the negative end is usually the crimped or marked end.
4
The unmarked lead is positiveAlso often the longer lead on a fresh, unbent part. Install backwards and the capacitor can heat up, bulge at the vent, or fail outright.
Tantalum capacitors are polarized too — but the marking convention flips: they usually mark the positive lead with a "+" or a stripe, the opposite convention from aluminium electrolytics. Always check for the + or − explicitly rather than assuming.

Capacitor Type Gallery

Different bodies, different internal construction, different reasons to reach for one over another. Each card shows a simplified cross-section of what's actually inside.

Does the reader above cover every marking system? It covers the three you'll meet on well over 90% of modern parts: the EIA 3-digit code, the SMD 2-digit + letter code, and direct-print values. A few older or specialized systems exist outside that: colour bands (a resistor-style 4–6 band system used on capacitors made before roughly the 1980s, now essentially obsolete but still found on vintage gear), silver mica dot codes (a similar band/dot system on old mica capacitors), and safety-class letters like X1/X2/Y1/Y2 or dielectric-family codes like C0G/NP0, X7R, Y5V — these don't encode capacitance at all, they describe temperature stability or mains-safety rating alongside a normal value code.

Charging, Discharging & AC vs. DC Behavior

A capacitor's job is to store energy in an electric field between two plates. How fast it fills up, how much it holds, and whether it can survive alternating current all come down to its internal construction.

On a DC supply

Current rushes in first, then tapers off as the capacitor's voltage climbs toward the supply voltage — an exponential curve, not a straight line. Once fully charged, a capacitor on steady DC draws essentially no current: it has effectively blocked the DC. Disconnect the supply and it discharges the same way in reverse.

On an AC supply

Because AC keeps reversing direction, the capacitor never finishes charging one way before it's asked to charge the other — so current appears to flow through it continuously. Its opposition to that current (reactance) drops as frequency rises, which is why capacitors are used to pass AC while blocking DC in coupling and filter circuits.

The polarity warning that actually matters: non-polarized types (ceramic, film, and AC-rated motor run capacitors) don't care which way current flows — that's exactly why motor run capacitors can sit directly across mains AC for years. Electrolytic and tantalum capacitors are different: their dielectric is a microscopically thin oxide layer that only exists because it was electrochemically formed in one direction. Apply reverse voltage or raw AC and that oxide layer breaks down — the capacitor can short, overheat, vent, or in tantalum's case, fail violently. Never wire a standard polarized capacitor across an AC line.

Behavior at a glance

Response speed reflects typical ESR (equivalent series resistance) — low ESR means the capacitor can charge, discharge, and react to fast signals with less internal loss.
