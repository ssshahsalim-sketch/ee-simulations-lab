What is a Kirk Key?

The foundation concept behind every simulation in this application.

DEFINITION

A Kirk Key interlock (also called a trapped-key or captive-key interlock) is a purely mechanical safety system that uses uniquely coded keys and matching lock cylinders to force switchgear — breakers, isolators, earth switches, transformer taps, doors — to be operated in one and only one physically permitted sequence.

Unlike an electrical interlock (auxiliary contacts + relay logic), a trapped-key interlock cannot be defeated by a control-power failure, a wiring fault, or a software bug. The key itself is the permission token, and it can only exist in one place at a time. That single fact — one key, one place, one permission — is the whole idea. Everything else in this app is a variation on it.

WHY IT EXISTS

In switchgear, some combinations of breaker/isolator states are dangerous or destructive: paralleling two unsynchronized sources, energising a bus while its earth switch is closed, back-feeding a transformer, or closing a tie breaker between two live, out-of-phase buses. Electrical interlocks can enforce this in normal operation, but maintenance, testing and emergency conditions need a safety layer that still works with the control supply dead. Kirk key systems provide that layer.

HOW A SEQUENCE IS FORCED

Every lock in a scheme is either free (bolt retracted, its equipment may operate, key captive) or blocked (bolt extended into the equipment's mechanism, equipment forced to the safe state, key removable). Moving a key from lock A to lock B is only mechanically possible if the operator first drives lock A to the state that releases the key — which itself typically requires the associated breaker to be open. This is why "Insert → Rotate → Operate → Rotate back → Remove → Transfer" is the universal action sequence you'll use throughout this simulator.

Transfer Block & Key Exchange Box

Two hardware items that let trapped-key logic scale beyond simple lock-to-lock pairs.

TRANSFER BLOCK

A transfer block is a small captive-key unit with two locks free-standing on a wall or panel plate — not attached to any breaker. Its only job is to swap a key's "identity." A key is inserted and trapped in lock 1, which releases a different key from lock 2. This lets a single physical action bridge between two otherwise unrelated key systems, or let one key "stand in" for a state confirmation without being wired to any specific breaker mechanism.

KEY EXCHANGE BOX

An exchange box (multi-lock box) holds several keys captive simultaneously, and only releases them — usually one at a time, or in a fixed group — once a specific "master" or "release" key has been inserted and rotated. Exchange boxes are the standard way to manage large ring-main or multi-breaker schemes (5–20+ keys) where a simple lock-to-lock chain would be unmanageable. Think of it as a mechanical logic gate for keys.

Main–Tie–Main Interlock

Main A, Tie Breaker, Main B. Two keys (K1, K2). The Tie breaker has two locks of its own and can only close once BOTH keys have been transferred to it — meaning both Mains must first be opened. This prevents paralleling either Main through the Tie and prevents all three breakers being closed together.
