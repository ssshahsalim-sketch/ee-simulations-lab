What this tool covers

MRI doesn't use radiation — it listens to hydrogen protons talking back to a magnetic field.

A clinical scanner is four systems working together: a huge static magnet, three gradient coils that encode position, an RF coil that excites and listens to tissue, and a computer that turns the received signal into a cross-sectional image via 2D Fourier reconstruction. This tool walks through each system, then lets you run an actual (small, real-math) scan on a sample patient.

Signal chain

The four systems

Main Magnet (B₀)

A superconducting coil, bathed in liquid helium at ~4K, produces a constant field of 1.5T or 3T — roughly 30,000–60,000× Earth's field. It aligns hydrogen protons in the body along its axis.

Gradient Coils

Three coil sets (X, Y, Z) add small, rapidly-switched field variations along each axis. They make resonance frequency depend on position, which is how the scanner locates signal in 3D space.

RF Coil

A birdcage-shaped transmit/receive coil fires precisely-timed radio pulses that tip proton spins away from alignment, then listens for the faint RF signal they emit while relaxing back.

Reconstruction Computer

Raw signal is stored in "k-space" — a frequency-domain grid. A 2D inverse Fourier transform of that grid produces the anatomical image radiologists read.

Why MRI, compared to X-ray / CT

No ionizing radiation

MRI uses magnetic fields and radio waves, not X-rays, so it's preferred for repeated imaging, pregnancy-sensitive cases, and pediatric studies where cumulative radiation dose matters.

Superior soft-tissue contrast

Different soft tissues (white/grey brain matter, cartilage, ligament, tumour vs healthy tissue) have distinct T1/T2 relaxation times, giving contrast that CT and X-ray usually cannot match.

Trade-offs

Scans take longer (typically 15–45 min), cost more, are noisy, and are contraindicated for certain implants — covered in the Safety tab. CT remains faster and better for acute bone/trauma imaging.

The Larmor equation

Every proton spins on its own axis. Placed in field B₀, it doesn't just align — it precesses around B₀ like a spinning top precesses around gravity, at a frequency set only by field strength and the nucleus:

f = γ · B₀

For hydrogen, γ = 42.58 MHz/T. This frequency is why MRI uses radio waves — at clinical field strengths it lands squarely in the RF band.

Tipping the magnetisation

At equilibrium, billions of proton spins sum to one net magnetisation vector M, aligned with B₀ (longitudinal, along Z). An RF pulse at exactly the Larmor frequency — the resonance condition — tips M away from Z by the flip angle, determined by pulse strength × duration.

  • 90° pulse: puts all magnetisation into the transverse (X-Y) plane — maximum signal, used for spin-echo T1/T2 imaging.
  • Small flip angles (10–30°): used in fast gradient-echo sequences, leaving more longitudinal magnetisation for rapid repeats.
  • 180° pulse: fully inverts M — used for refocusing echoes and for fat/fluid suppression (inversion recovery).
Relaxation curves
T2 decay (transverse Mxy) T1 recovery (longitudinal Mz)

Why images have contrast at all

Once tipped into the transverse plane, M does two things simultaneously, at rates unique to each tissue:

  • T1 recovery — Mz regrows back toward B₀ alignment as spins dump energy to the surrounding lattice ("spin-lattice").
  • T2 decay — Mxy shrinks as spins dephase relative to each other from local field interactions ("spin-spin"), always faster than T1.

The scan operator chooses TR (repetition time) and TE (echo time) to sample these curves at the moment that best separates the tissues of interest — that choice is what "T1-weighted" or "T2-weighted" actually means.

Slice / phase / frequency encoding

Turning a 3D signal into a 3D coordinate

Every proton in the body is precessing — the RF coil hears them all at once as a single mixed signal. Gradients make that separable by position:

  • Slice-select gradient (Gz): applied during the RF pulse, so only protons in one thin slice happen to be at the Larmor frequency and get excited.
  • Phase-encode gradient (Gy): applied briefly before readout, leaves each row with a different phase twist — repeated on every TR to fill each k-space line.
  • Frequency-encode gradient (Gx): applied during readout, so each column resonates at a different frequency, read out simultaneously.

The result is a grid of raw signal — k-space — where each point encodes one spatial frequency of the final image. See it built and inverted live in the Scan Simulator tab.

The magnet is always on

Critical fact: the superconducting main magnet is never switched off — not between patients, not overnight, not during a power cut. Ramping it down (a "quench") is a deliberate emergency-only procedure. This is the source of almost every MRI safety rule.

The projectile effect

Why it happens

Field strength rises steeply near the bore. A loose ferromagnetic object (oxygen cylinder, wheelchair, scissors, pen) even several metres away can be pulled in with lethal force and speed once it's within a few metres — this is the single most common cause of serious MRI accidents.

All staff and patients pass a ferromagnetic screening checklist before entering Zone III/IV.

Zone system (ACR guidance)

Zone I

Freely accessible public area — outside any MRI-related hazard, e.g. building reception.

Zone II

Interface between public and controlled areas — patient reception, screening, changing.

Zone III

Restricted access, physically secured — only screened, authorised personnel and patients.

Zone IV

The scanner room itself — always inside Zone III, marked, with warning lights, entry only under supervision.

Absolute & conditional contraindications

Typically absolute

  • Non-MRI-conditional cardiac pacemakers / ICDs
  • Certain older aneurysm clips (ferromagnetic)
  • Cochlear implants (unless MRI-conditional)
  • Retained ferromagnetic shrapnel/metal near critical structures (e.g. the eye)

Conditional / needs review

  • Most modern orthopaedic implants, stents, joint replacements (usually MRI-safe/conditional — verify device documentation)
  • Tattoos with metallic pigment (rare mild heating)
  • Pregnancy — generally avoided in the first trimester unless clinically necessary
  • Severe claustrophobia — managed with open-bore scanners or sedation

Other operating hazards

Acoustic noise

Rapid gradient switching causes loud banging/knocking (up to ~110–120 dB) from Lorentz-force vibration in the coils — hearing protection is mandatory for patients and staff.

RF heating (SAR)

RF pulses deposit energy as heat. Specific Absorption Rate (SAR) is monitored and limited per IEC 60601-2-33 to prevent tissue heating, especially relevant with implants or long/high-field scans.

Quench & cryogen venting

A quench (sudden loss of superconductivity) boils off liquid helium almost instantly; the room's quench pipe vents it outside. If it vents into the room, oxygen displacement is an asphyxiation risk — rooms include oxygen monitors.

Standards referenced: IEC 60601-2-33 (particular requirements for MRI equipment safety), ACR Manual on MR Safety, IEC 62464-1 (image quality), NEMA MS standards for performance measurement.

Glossary

TR / TE

TR (repetition time): time between successive excitation pulses on the same slice. TE (echo time): time from excitation to signal readout. Together they set which relaxation curve dominates image contrast.

k-space

The raw spatial-frequency data matrix collected by the scanner. Its centre holds contrast/low-frequency information; its edges hold fine detail/edges. An inverse 2D Fourier transform turns it into the viewable image.

Spin echo vs gradient echo

Spin-echo sequences use a 180° refocusing pulse to cancel field-inhomogeneity dephasing (true T2). Gradient-echo sequences skip that pulse — faster, but sensitive to T2* effects (e.g. bleeding, calcification).

FLAIR

Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery — a T2-weighted sequence with an added inversion pulse tuned to null (blacken) CSF signal, making periventricular lesions far easier to see.
