What you're seeing

A radio wave is a travelling electromagnetic oscillation — a self-propagating disturbance of coupled electric (E) and magnetic (H) fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. The animation shows the electric field component along the direction of propagation. Everything about a radio signal — its range, how it bends around the Earth, whether it bounces off the ionosphere, how big its antenna needs to be — falls out of the single relationship c = fλ.

The Full Electromagnetic Spectrum

Radio waves are the lowest-frequency, longest-wavelength slice of the EM spectrum — from sub-audio ELF waves thousands of kilometres long, up to millimetre-wave signals bordering infrared. Everything in this band obeys the same Maxwell's-equations physics as visible light; only f and λ differ.

How each mode behaves

Ground wave — diffracts along the Earth's curved, conductive surface. Dominant below ~2 MHz (LF/MF). Range from tens to ~1000 km depending on ground conductivity and power; energy is absorbed by the ground, so range shrinks as frequency rises.
Sky wave — launched upward, refracted/reflected back by the ionosphere's F-layer (and E/D in daytime). This is how HF shortwave crosses continents and oceans by "bouncing" between ionosphere and ground in multiple hops. Only works below the Maximum Usable Frequency (MUF); above it, the wave punches through into space.
Space wave — direct line-of-sight plus a ground-reflected ray, used by VHF/UHF/microwave. Range is limited by the radio horizon (Earth's curvature blocks it beyond that), which is why FM/TV/cellular need tall towers or many repeaters, unlike HF.

Two copies of the same signal — the direct ray and the ground/ionosphere-reflected ray — arrive at the receiver having travelled slightly different distances. Depending on that path difference, they add up constructively (stronger signal) or destructively (deep fade). This is why signal strength flickers as you (or a reflecting object) move even a few centimetres at high frequencies — classic multipath fading, the same effect behind Wi-Fi dead spots and mobile "cell edge" dropouts.

Physics & Research Notes

Core wave relationship

c = f × λ  (c = 2.998×10⁸ m/s in vacuum)

Frequency (f, in Hz) is how many oscillations pass a point per second. Wavelength (λ, in metres) is the physical distance between successive wave crests. Velocity (v) is how fast the wavefront travels through the medium. In any medium, v = c/n where n is the refractive index; since the transmitter fixes f, a slower v (denser medium) always means a shorter λ.

Ionospheric reflection (sky wave)

f₀ = 9√Nᶫᶍᶎ  ·  MUF = f₀ × sec(θ)

The ionosphere is a region (roughly 60–500 km up) ionised by solar UV/X-rays into D, E, and F layers of free electrons. f₀ is the critical frequency: the highest frequency reflected straight back at vertical incidence, set by the peak electron density Nᶫᶍᶎ of the layer. Sent at an oblique angle θ from vertical, higher frequencies can still be bent back — up to the Maximum Usable Frequency. Above the MUF for a given path, the wave refracts too little and escapes into space instead of returning to Earth. The D-layer exists only in daylight (recombines after sunset) and mainly absorbs MF/lower-HF energy rather than reflecting it — which is exactly why AM broadcast stations are audible over far greater distances at night once the D-layer disappears and MF sky-wave propagation becomes possible.

Space wave / radio horizon

d(km) ≈ 4.12 × (√hᶍ + √hᵣ)   [heights in metres]

This accounts for standard atmospheric refraction (effective Earth radius factor k≈4/3, which bends VHF/UHF rays slightly around the true curvature). Beyond this distance, the direct ray is geometrically blocked by the Earth's bulge — the fundamental reason VHF/UHF/microwave systems need height (tall towers, satellites, or repeater chains) while HF does not.

Ground wave

A vertically-polarised LF/MF wave induces currents in the conductive ground that re-radiate energy slightly forward, letting the wavefront diffract around the curvature rather than travelling in a strict straight line. Attenuation increases sharply with frequency and with poor ground conductivity (dry/rocky ground attenuates far more than seawater), which is why ground-wave range is only practically useful below roughly 2–3 MHz.

Two-ray multipath model

Δpath ≈ 2hᶍhᵣ/d   ·   Δφ = (2π/λ)Δpath + φᵣᶍᶿ   ·  r₁(Fresnel) = √(λdᶍdᵣ/(dᶍ+dᵣ))

The classic flat-earth two-ray model: a direct ray and a single ground-reflected ray combine at the receiver. The reflected ray picks up a path-length difference Δpath and, for a grazing-incidence ground reflection, an additional ~180° phase flip. Where the two arrive in phase, signal reinforces (up to +6 dB); where they arrive out of phase, they can nearly cancel — deep fades. The first Fresnel zone radius marks the region around the direct path where obstructions or reflectors most strongly affect the link, used in real antenna/tower siting to keep line-of-sight paths clear.

Scope & simplifications

This tool is built for intuition-building, using standard textbook approximations (flat/curved-Earth two-ray geometry, simplified ionosphere electron-density-to-critical-frequency relation, constant refractive indices). Real propagation also depends on solar activity, season, terrain, polarisation, transmitter power, antenna radiation pattern, and atmospheric ducting — not modelled here. For licensed RF link-budget work, cross-check against ITU-R P-series recommendations (P.368 ground wave, P.533 HF sky-wave prediction, P.1546 point-to-area VHF/UHF) and dedicated propagation-prediction software.
