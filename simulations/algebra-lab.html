The Square of a Sum

(a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b²

A square with side (a+b) splits perfectly into four pieces: an a×a square, a b×b square, and two a×b rectangles. Their areas always add up to the big square — that's the whole identity.

The Square of a Difference

(a−b)² = a² − 2ab + b²

Start from a big a×a square. Carve a (a−b)×(a−b) square from one corner, and what's left is two a×b-ish strips plus a leftover b×b square — rearranged, they show why subtracting b twice still leaves +b².

Difference of Squares

a² − b² = (a−b)(a+b)

Cut a b×b square out of the corner of an a×a square. The leftover L-shape (the "gnomon") has area a²−b². Slice that L along the bend and it re-tiles, with zero waste, into a clean rectangle of width (a−b) and height (a+b).

Squaring Three Terms

(a+b+c)² = a²+b²+c²+2ab+2bc+2ca

Same idea, one more row and column. A (a+b+c)×(a+b+c) square breaks into a 3×3 grid: three squares on the diagonal (a², b², c²) and six matching rectangles off it, each pair worth 2·(product).

Multiplying Two Binomials

(a+b)(a+c) = a² + a(b+c) + bc

A rectangle of width (a+b) and height (a+c) splits into four smaller rectangles. Multiplying two binomials is just adding up the areas of that 2×2 grid — nothing more mysterious than that.

The Quadratic Formula

x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac)) / 2a

The formula just locates where the parabola y = ax²+bx+c crosses the x-axis. The discriminant b²−4ac tells you, before you even plot anything, whether there are two crossings, one, or none.

Exponent Rules, as Stacks

aᵐ · aⁿ = a^(m+n)

Think of aᵐ as m identical bars of length a, stacked end to end (multiplied together). Multiplying by aⁿ just stacks n more bars on top — the exponents add because the stacks combine.

Modulus on a Number Line

|ab| = |a|·|b|

|a| is just distance from zero — direction doesn't matter, so the bar always points right. Multiply two numbers and the distances multiply too, no matter which signs you started with.
