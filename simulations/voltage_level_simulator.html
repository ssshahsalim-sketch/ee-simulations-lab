🎚 Pick a power and a distance — let the grid choose its own voltage

Every transmission voltage in use today (11 kV up to 800 kV) exists because someone balanced two costs that move in opposite directions as voltage rises: conductor / loss cost falls, while insulation, tower and switchgear cost rises. The classic approximation for the economic voltage was published by Still, and is still taught in every power-systems course:

V(kV, line) = 5.5 × √( 0.62 × L(km) + P(kW) / 100 )

This gives the economical voltage — the point where extra insulation cost is just repaid by lower conductor/loss cost. Utilities then round up to the nearest standard voltage class, because you can't buy a "119 kV" transformer.

🔥 Line Losses Laboratory

Transmission loss is dissipated as heat in the conductor's resistance: Ploss = 3 I² R. Since I = P / (√3·V·pf), doubling the voltage for the same power roughly quarters the current — and loss falls with the square of current. That single fact is the entire reason EHV/UHV grids exist.

🧮 Why the per-unit (p.u.) system exists

A real transmission network has several different voltage levels stitched together by transformers — 11 kV at the generator, 220 kV on the line, 33 kV at the load. Comparing raw ohms across these zones means multiplying by the transformer's turns-ratio² every single time. The per-unit system removes that bookkeeping: every quantity is expressed as a fraction of a chosen base value, and if the base voltages are assigned correctly (following each transformer's ratio), the per-unit impedance of a transformer is the same number seen from either side.

Base I =
Sbase(kVA) / (√3 · Vbase(kV))
Base Z =
Vbase²(kV) / Sbase(MVA)
Zp.u. =
Zactual / Zbase

To move an impedance already in per-unit from its own nameplate base onto a common system base:

Zp.u.,new = Zp.u.,old × (Sbase,new / Srated) × (Vrated / Vbase,new)²

📐 Formula reference

Economic transmission voltage (Still's formula)

V(kV) = 5.5 √( 0.62·L(km) + P(kW)/100 )

Three-phase line current

I = P / (√3 · VL · cosφ)

Transmission loss

Ploss = 3 I² Rtotal    where Rtotal = r(Ω/km) × L(km)

Approximate voltage drop (short-line, R dominant or R+X)

ΔV ≈ √3 · I · (R cosφ + X sinφ)

Per-unit base quantities

Ibase = Sbase(kVA) / (√3 Vbase(kV))    Zbase = Vbase²(kV)/Sbase(MVA)    Zp.u. = Zactual/Zbase

🕰 Why these particular numbers — 11 · 22 · 33 · 66 · 132 · 220 · 400 · 800 kV?

1

11 kV is the generator-friendly starting point. Alternator stator insulation and winding economics historically settled around this level — it's still the standard generation and industrial distribution voltage in the IS/IEC world.

2

22, 33 kV double the base for the earliest transmission/sub-transmission tiers, keeping transformer ratios simple whole multiples of the generation voltage.

3

66, 132, 220 kV emerged as the sub-transmission and transmission tiers of the growing regional grid — each roughly a 2× step, matched to the reach and load growth of the era's cities and industrial belts.

4

400 kV became the backbone EHV (extra-high-voltage) tier once inter-state, inter-regional bulk power flow was needed — carrying hundreds of MW economically over hundreds of km.

5

800 kV is UHV (ultra-high-voltage) territory, reserved for the very long, very heavy corridors (large hydro/thermal clusters to distant load centres). At this level corona loss, audible noise and insulation coordination — not conductor cost — become the limiting design factors, and towers/right-of-way costs dominate the economics from Still's formula.

Corona loss (not modelled in Tab 2's I²R figure) becomes significant above ~220 kV and is a major reason EHV/UHV lines use bundled conductors (2, 4, 6, even 8 sub-conductors per phase) — which is also why the illustrative resistance per km keeps dropping sharply in the table.

⚠️ On the numbers used in this tool

The conductor resistance, typical distance range and typical power range shown per voltage class are illustrative planning figures commonly used for teaching, not a substitute for an actual conductor datasheet or a load-flow/short-circuit study. Real designs follow IS 802 (overhead line design), CBIP manuals, CEA transmission planning criteria and the specific conductor's published parameters (e.g. ACSR Moose, Zebra, Panther codes), and account for corona, thermal rating, sag-tension and right-of-way constraints that this simplified tool does not model.
