What Is Arc Flash?

An arc flash is a thermal explosion released when a fault current jumps through air between conductors (or from a conductor to ground) instead of flowing through the intended path. Unlike a simple electric shock, which needs a completed circuit through the body, an arc flash can injure someone who never touches anything — it's the released heat, pressure wave, and molten metal spray that cause harm.

A bolted three-phase fault ionises the air. Once ionised, air conducts almost like a wire, and the arc can sustain itself until a protective device interrupts it. Temperatures inside the arc plasma can reach roughly 19,000°C — about four times hotter than the surface of the sun — for the fraction of a second the arc burns.

Protective Device Impact — Why Fast Tripping Matters

Incident energy is directly proportional to arc duration. The same fault, cleared quickly by a well-coordinated relay/breaker versus slowly by an upstream or miscoordinated device, can be the difference between PPE Category 1 and a fatality. Why This Happens

Devices that reduce arc duration directly reduce incident energy: current-limiting fuses, properly coordinated instantaneous relay elements, differential (87) protection, arc-flash-relay light/current sensing schemes, and maintenance-mode switches that temporarily lower an instantaneous pickup while work is performed nearby. This is the same protective-device logic used across the relay simulators in this suite — the speed of the trip decision is a safety variable, not just a reliability one.

Formulas & Standards Reference

All calculations in this tool use the IEEE 1584-2002 empirical model, which remains widely taught and used for estimation. Site-specific studies should use the current IEEE 1584-2018 edition with full equipment class/box-size lookup tables and a qualified engineer's review.

1. Arcing Current

For systems < 1 kV:

lg(Ia) = K + 0.662·lg(Ibf) + 0.0966·V + 0.000526·G + 0.5588·V·lg(Ibf) − 0.00304·G·lg(Ibf)

For systems ≥ 1 kV:

lg(Ia) = 0.00402 + 0.983·lg(Ibf)

K = −0.153 for open air, −0.097 for a box/enclosure. V = system voltage (kV), G = gap (mm), Ibf = bolted fault current (kA).

2. Normalised Incident Energy (at 610 mm, 0.2 s)

lg(En) = K1 + K2 + 1.081·lg(Ia) + 0.0011·G

K1 = −0.792 (open air) or −0.555 (box). K2 = 0 (ungrounded/HRG) or −0.113 (grounded).

3. Incident Energy at Working Distance

E (cal/cm²) = 4.184 · Cf · En · (t / 0.2) · (610 / D)x

Cf = 1.5 for systems ≤ 1 kV, 1.0 above. t = arc duration (s), D = working distance (mm), x = distance exponent from the table below.

4. Arc Flash Boundary

DB (mm) = [4.184 · Cf · En · (t / 0.2) · 610x / EB]1/x

EB = 1.2 cal/cm², the accepted onset-of-second-degree-burn threshold used to define the boundary distance.
