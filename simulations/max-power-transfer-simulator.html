Where This Shows Up In Real Engineering

Bonus: Solar Panels & Maximum Power Point Tracking

A solar cell isn't a simple linear source — its voltage sags as you draw more current, tracing a curved I-V characteristic. Multiply voltage × current at every point and you get the power curve on the right (P = V × I), which peaks at one specific operating point: the Maximum Power Point (MPP).

This is the exact same theorem, just for a nonlinear source: there's one "load impedance" (effectively, the ratio V/I the charge controller presents to the panel) that extracts the most watts. Since sunlight intensity and temperature constantly shift where that peak sits, real solar charge controllers run an MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking) algorithm — commonly "Perturb & Observe": nudge the operating point slightly, see if power went up or down, and keep stepping in the direction that increases power. It's max power transfer theorem, solved in real time, thousands of times a second.

Physics & Research Notes

The theorem

Zᵟ = Zₛ*   (i.e. Rᵟ=Rₛ, Xᵟ=−Xₛ)

A source with internal (Thévenin) impedance Zₛ = Rₛ + jXₛ delivers maximum real power to a load Zᵟ when the load equals the source impedance's complex conjugate. The reactive parts must be equal and opposite (they cancel, so together they behave like a plain wire at that frequency) and the resistive parts must be equal.

Deriving it

Pᵟ = |I|²Rᵟ = Vₛ²Rᵟ / [(Rₛ+Rᵟ)² + (Xₛ+Xᵟ)²]

The reactive terms only ever increase the denominator, never help deliver power — so the first move is always to null them: set Xᵟ = −Xₛ, which maximises Pᵟ for any fixed Rᵟ. With the denominator reduced to (Rₛ+Rᵟ)², taking dPᵟ/dRᵟ = 0 gives the second condition, Rᵟ = Rₛ. Substituting both back gives the maximum deliverable power:

Pᵟ(max) = Vₛ² / (4Rₛ)

The efficiency trade-off (the part most people miss)

At the exact conjugate-match point, efficiency is exactly 50% — the source's own resistance Rₛ dissipates exactly as much power as the load receives, since Rᵟ=Rₛ means they share the current equally. Efficiency η = Rᵟ/(Rᵟ+Rₛ) keeps climbing toward 100% as Rᵟ grows past Rₛ, but Pᵟ itself falls off past the peak. Maximum power and maximum efficiency are different, competing goals — which is exactly why RF/signal systems chase conjugate match while power-grid systems deliberately avoid it (see Tab 3).

Reflection coefficient and why mismatches reflect power

Γ = (Zᵟ − Z₀)/(Zᵟ+Z₀)   ,   Pᵟ/Pᶍᶜᶝ = 1−|Γ|²

On a transmission line of real characteristic impedance Z₀, any load that isn't exactly Z₀ reflects a fraction |Γ|² of the incident power straight back toward the source — it physically cannot reach the load. Because Z₀ is real, "Zᵟ conjugate-matches Z₀" reduces to simply Zᵟ=Z₀, i.e. Γ=0. This is the mechanism: the theorem tells you the destination (zero mismatch loss), and the reflected wave is literally the energy that doesn't make it there when you fall short.

Standing waves and VSWR

VSWR = (1+|Γ|)/(1−|Γ|)

The incident and reflected waves superpose along the line, creating fixed points of reinforcement (voltage maxima) and cancellation (voltage minima) — a standing wave. VSWR quantifies how deep that pattern is: VSWR=1 (matched, no reflection) means a perfectly flat line with no standing wave at all; higher VSWR means a deeper, more pronounced pattern and more power turned away at the mismatch.

Why some systems deliberately avoid conjugate match

Because it caps efficiency at 50% right at the optimum, conjugate matching only makes sense when the signal itself is scarce and precious (weak antenna signals, sensor outputs, RF stages) — extracting the last available watt matters more than wasting the other half as heat. In power distribution, generators, and battery systems, power is abundant and heat is the enemy, so those systems intentionally keep load impedance much higher than source impedance and optimise for efficiency instead, accepting that they're nowhere near the theoretical maximum-power point.

Scope & simplifications

This tool treats sources as ideal Thévenin-equivalent AC sources with fixed, frequency-independent Rₛ/Xₛ, which is the standard textbook framing for this theorem. Real sources (antennas, amplifiers, solar cells) have impedance or I-V characteristics that vary with frequency, temperature, or operating point — which is exactly why techniques like MPPT (Tab 3) exist: to continuously re-find the matching point rather than assume it's fixed.
