Calculus Explorer — Differentiation & Integration

Two ideas explain almost everything that changes: differentiation finds how fast something is changing right now, and integration adds up tiny changes to find a total. Drag points, drag sliders, watch rectangles fill in and tangent lines snap into place — the graphs update live, and every idea is tied to something you can picture: a moving car, a charging capacitor, a spinning motor shaft.

1What is a derivative? — the slope of "right now"

Pick two points on a curve and draw a straight line through them (a secant line). Its slope is the average rate of change between those points. Now slide the second point closer and closer to the first — the gap h shrinks toward zero, the secant line rotates, and it settles onto the line that just grazes the curve at one point: the tangent line. Its slope is the derivative — the instantaneous rate of change.

f'(x) = limh→0   [ f(x+h) − f(x) ] / h

1What is an integral? — adding up infinitely many slivers

To find the area under a curve, slice the region into thin rectangles, add up their areas, and watch what happens as the rectangles get thinner (n → ∞). The sum settles on the exact area — the definite integral. This is the reverse process of differentiation: instead of finding a rate from a total, you're building a total out of rates.

∫ab f(x) dx = limn→∞   Σ f(xi) · Δx

1The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus — differentiation and integration are inverses

Everything above has been building to one idea. Define an accumulation function F(x) = area under f from a fixed start point up to x. As x moves right, F(x) is exactly the running total (integration). Now ask: how fast is that running total changing? Its slope, at every point, equals the height of f at that point. In other words — taking the derivative undoes the integral, and vice versa.

F(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt   ⟹   F'(x) = f(x)

1RC Circuit — charging a capacitor

A capacitor charging through a resistor is the cleanest electrical demonstration of both operations at once. Voltage builds up as v(t) = Vs(1 − e−t/RC). Current is how fast charge is being pushed in — the derivative of voltage. Charge stored is the running total of that current — its integral.

i(t) = C · dv/dt   (differentiation)     q(t) = ∫ i(t) dt   (integration)

2Rotating shaft — angle, angular speed & a tachometer

A motor shaft's angle θ(t) is tracked by an encoder. Angular velocity ω(t) — what a tachometer reads — is the derivative dθ/dt. Run the tachometer reading forward through time and integrate it, and you recover total angle turned (how many revolutions have happened) — the integral of ω.

ω(t) = dθ/dt   (differentiation)     θ(t) = ∫ ω(t) dt   (integration)

3More derivative ↔ integral pairs you meet on the job

Water tank filling integration

Inflow rate r(t) (L/min) integrated over time gives total volume in the tank. The flow meter reads a derivative; the tank level is the running integral.

Inductor differentiation

v(t) = L·di/dt — inductor voltage is proportional to the derivative of current. Sudden current changes (large di/dt) produce voltage spikes — the reason for flyback diodes across relay coils.

Power & energy integration

Energy E = ∫P dt. Your electricity meter (kWh) is a physical integrator, continuously summing instantaneous power draw over the billing period.

Vibration & acceleration differentiation

Velocity is the derivative of position; acceleration is the derivative of velocity. An accelerometer measures the second derivative directly — used in motor bearing condition monitoring.

Thermal heating integration

Temperature rise in a winding under constant I²R loss accumulates over time until it reaches thermal equilibrium — the same charging-curve shape as the RC capacitor above.

Load profile & demand integration

Maximum demand billing integrates power over a sliding 15/30-minute window — utilities are, quite literally, running a Riemann sum on your load.

4Riemann sum methods — why they converge differently

Left / right rectangles use the function value at the start or end of each slice — simple, but biased high or low whenever the curve is consistently rising or falling across the interval. Midpoint rectangles sample the centre of each slice, cancelling much of that bias. Trapezoids connect the two endpoints of each slice with a straight line instead of a flat top, tracking curvature even better. All four methods converge to the same exact integral as n → ∞ — they just get there at different rates, which matters a great deal in numerical/embedded computation where n is limited by CPU time.

Whenever an instrument reads a rate (speedometer, ammeter across a capacitor, tachometer, flow meter), something else in the system is quietly acting as its integral (odometer, coulomb counter, revolution counter, tank level) — and vice versa.
