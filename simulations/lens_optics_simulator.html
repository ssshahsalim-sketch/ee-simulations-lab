Physics & Research Notes — What This Simulator Actually Computes

This tool does not use idealised thin-lens ray diagrams alone — every ray is geometrically intersected with the real spherical (or flat) lens surfaces and refracted using the vector form of Snell's Law, so genuine effects like spherical aberration, edge-ray bending and chromatic spreading emerge naturally from the geometry, the same way they do in a real lens.

1. Surface Geometry (Cartesian sign convention)

Light travels left → right (+x). For a spherical surface of signed radius R with vertex at x₀, the surface height-to-position relation used for ray intersection is:
x(y) = x₀ + R − sign(R)·√(R² − y²)
R > 0 means the centre of curvature lies to the right of the vertex (surface bulges toward −x / the incoming light). This single convention correctly generates biconvex, biconcave, plano- and meniscus forms just by choosing the signs and magnitudes of R1 and R2 — no special-casing per lens type.

2. Refraction — Vector Snell's Law

At each surface hit, the local normal N and incident direction d (unit vectors) give the refracted direction directly, without decomposing into 2D angles first:
t = (n1/n2)d + ( (n1/n2)cosθᵢ − cosθₜ )·N
cosθₜ = √(1 − (n1/n2)²(1 − cosθᵢ²))
If the term under the root is negative, the ray undergoes Total Internal Reflection (TIR) instead of refracting — this simulator detects that condition at every surface, not just the exit face.

3. Partial Reflection — Fresnel Equations

Every real interface reflects some light even when it isn't totally internally reflected. Rather than the full polarisation-dependent Fresnel equations, this tool uses Schlick's approximation (a well-known 1994 fast approximation used in real-time optics/graphics engines) for unpolarised reflectance, which is accurate to within a fraction of a percent for typical glass/air interfaces:
R0 = ((n1−n2)/(n1+n2))²
R(θ) = R0 + (1−R0)(1−cosθ)⁵
Toggle "Surface Reflections" to render these secondary rays — their brightness is scaled by the computed R(θ), and you'll notice reflectance climbing sharply near grazing incidence at the lens edge, exactly as it does on real glass.

4. Chromatic Dispersion — Cauchy's Equation

Refractive index is wavelength-dependent. This tool approximates each material's dispersion with the classic two-term Cauchy equation, calibrated so n(589 nm) equals the material's specified index:
n(λ) = A + B / λ²
With "Chromatic Dispersion" on, each ray is split into seven representative visible wavelengths (700 nm red → 400 nm violet); violet light has a higher index and bends more, spreading the beam exactly the way a prism or a real lens edge does.

5. Lensmaker's Equation (thick-lens form)

The focal length readout is not the simplified thin-lens formula — it includes the thickness correction term, valid for real, finitely-thick lenses:
1/f = (n−1) · [ 1/R1 − 1/R2 + ((n−1)·t) / (n·R1·R2) ]
A converging lens has f > 0; a diverging lens has f < 0. Power P = 1/f (in mm⁻¹ here; multiply by 1000 for dioptres if f is in metres).

6. Image Formation

The Image Formation tab uses the standard Gaussian thin-lens relation and magnification formula alongside the real ray trace, so you can directly compare the idealised prediction with reality:
1/v − 1/u = 1/f m = v / u
u is negative (object to the left, real object). A positive v places a real, inverted image on the transmission side; a negative v is a virtual, erect image on the same side as the object — exactly what a magnifying glass shows when the object is inside the focal length.

7. Known Simplifications

For clarity this is a 2D meridional cross-section (not a full 3D skew-ray trace), coatings/AR layers are not modelled (so Fresnel reflectance is the bare-glass value), and multiple internal bounces are not iterated beyond one reflection per surface. Aperture, radii and thickness are schematic engineering values, not calibrated to a specific catalogue lens. Sign convention and formulas follow standard optical-engineering references (cf. Hecht, Optics; drawing conventions loosely follow ISO 10110 practice for optical components).
