How Bluetooth actually moves data, why it feels slow, and how apps like Nearby Share / AirDrop / Xender scan for devices and still transfer fast.

The radio layer

Bluetooth runs in the unlicensed 2.4 GHz ISM band, the same band as Wi-Fi and microwave ovens. To avoid colliding with other radios in that crowded band, it uses Adaptive Frequency Hopping (AFH): classic Bluetooth hops across 79 channels (1 MHz apart), Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) across 40 channels (2 MHz apart), switching channel roughly 1,600 times a second for Classic. This is great for reliability in noisy environments, but every hop, sync, and re-lock costs time that a fixed-channel radio like Wi-Fi doesn't pay.

Pairing, in short

  • Devices advertise (BLE) or become discoverable (Classic) so others can find them by scanning.
  • A pairing exchange negotiates a shared link key, often with a PIN, passkey, or "Just Works" for simple accessories.
  • Once bonded, the key is stored so future connections skip re-pairing and jump straight to an encrypted link.

Real throughput vs. the marketing number

The number printed on a spec sheet is the air data rate — the raw bit rate of the modulation. What you actually get after overhead, retransmits, and sleep intervals is much lower:

Where the time actually goes

  • Small packets: pre-4.2 BLE capped payloads at 20 bytes per packet; even BLE 5's "Data Length Extension" only reaches 251 bytes. Every packet still needs a header, CRC, and an acknowledgement.
  • Connection intervals: BLE devices agree to talk only every 7.5 ms–4 s and sleep in between to save power. Fewer wake-ups per second directly caps how much data can move — power efficiency and speed are a deliberate trade-off, not a bug.
  • Frequency hopping overhead: re-syncing the hop sequence after interference costs airtime that a fixed-channel link never spends.
  • Single active link, half-duplex-ish behaviour: no channel bonding or MIMO like Wi-Fi has — one narrow 1–2 MHz channel carries everything, one packet at a time.
  • Shared spectrum: Wi-Fi, microwaves, USB3 emissions, and other Bluetooth devices all crowd the same 2.4 GHz band.
  • It was never designed for bulk transfer: Bluetooth's original brief was cable replacement for headsets, mice, and sensors — not moving gigabyte video files.

The trick: Bluetooth only makes the introduction

Apps like Google Nearby Share / Quick Share, Apple AirDrop, Xender, and ShareIt feel instant partly because the actual bulk data almost never travels over Bluetooth. Bluetooth (usually BLE) is only used to find nearby devices and swap a tiny handshake — then the real transfer happens over a direct Wi-Fi link, which is 10–100x faster.

A practical tool you can actually use

A browser can't drive real Bluetooth/Wi-Fi Direct device discovery and file transfer the way a native app can — the Web Bluetooth API is limited to specific accessory types and isn't a general file-transfer channel. For genuinely moving files fast between your own devices, these are the real practical options:

  • LocalSend (free, open-source, Windows/Android/iOS/Linux/Mac) — scans your local Wi-Fi network and transfers files directly, no cloud, no cable.
  • Snapdrop (snapdrop.net) — works entirely in the browser over your local Wi-Fi, no install needed, good for a quick PC-to-phone transfer.
  • Google Quick Share / Apple AirDrop — built into Android and iOS/macOS respectively, same BLE-discover-then-Wi-Fi-transfer approach described above.
