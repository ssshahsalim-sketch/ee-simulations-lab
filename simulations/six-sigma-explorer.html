What is Six Sigma?

Six Sigma is a data-driven methodology for reducing process variation and defects. "Sigma" (σ) is the standard deviation — a statistical measure of how much a process output varies from its mean. A "Six Sigma" process is one where the spread of the process is so tight that the nearest specification limit sits six standard deviations from the mean, which — accounting for a realistic 1.5σ long-term process shift — corresponds to about 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO).

Six Sigma combines the statistical rigor of quality control with the waste-elimination philosophy of Lean manufacturing (together often called Lean Six Sigma), and is deployed through structured, belt-ranked practitioners working on chartered improvement projects.

YELLOW BELT Core Vocabulary

Voice of the Customer (VOC)

The customer's stated and unstated needs, translated into measurable Critical to Quality (CTQ) characteristics that the process must satisfy.

Defect, Defective, Opportunity

A defect is any single non-conformance; a unit with one or more defects is defective. An opportunity is any characteristic on a unit that could fail to meet a CTQ requirement — this count is the denominator in DPMO.

COPQ — Cost of Poor Quality

The total cost of internal failures (scrap, rework), external failures (returns, warranty), appraisal (inspection), and prevention. Six Sigma projects are usually chartered around reducing COPQ.

5 Whys

Ask "why" repeatedly (typically five times) starting from a problem statement to drill down from a symptom to its root cause, rather than stopping at the first plausible explanation.

GREEN BELT Core Statistics

Central tendency & spread

Mean (average), median (middle value), mode (most frequent) describe where data centers. Range, variance, and standard deviation (σ) describe how spread out it is — σ is the language Six Sigma is named after.

σ = √( Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ⁄ (n − 1) )

The Normal Distribution

Many continuous process outputs approximate a bell-shaped normal distribution. About 68.3% of values fall within ±1σ of the mean, 95.5% within ±2σ, and 99.7% within ±3σ — the basis for 3-sigma control limits.

Process Capability — Cp, Cpk, Pp, Ppk

Capability indices compare the voice of the process (its natural spread) against the voice of the customer (the specification limits).

Cp = (USL − LSL) ⁄ 6σ   (potential capability — ignores centering)
Cpk = min[ (USL − x̄)⁄3σ , (x̄ − LSL)⁄3σ ]   (actual capability — accounts for centering)

Cp = Cpk means the process is perfectly centered between the limits. Cpk < Cp means the process is capable but off-center. Pp/Ppk use the same formulas but with the overall (long-term) standard deviation instead of the within-subgroup (short-term) one.

Control Charts (SPC)

Statistical Process Control charts plot a statistic over time against a centerline and control limits set at ±3σ of that statistic's own sampling distribution — not the spec limits. A point outside the limits, or a run/trend pattern, signals a special cause of variation worth investigating, as opposed to ordinary common cause variation the process always has.

Measurement System Analysis (MSA) & Gage R&R

Before trusting process data, Green Belts verify the measurement system itself isn't the biggest source of variation. Repeatability is variation when one operator measures the same part repeatedly; reproducibility is variation between different operators measuring the same part. A Gage R&R study apportions total observed variation into part-to-part, repeatability, and reproducibility components — commonly summarized as %Gage R&R of total tolerance, where <10% is acceptable, 10–30% may be acceptable, and >30% is unacceptable.

Basic Hypothesis Testing

A hypothesis test checks whether an observed difference (e.g., before vs. after an improvement) is statistically real or just random noise. The null hypothesis (H₀) assumes no real difference; the alternative (H₁) assumes there is one. A p-value below the chosen significance level (commonly α = 0.05) means the observed data would be unlikely under H₀, so H₀ is rejected.

BLACK BELT Design of Experiments (DOE)

Where Green Belt analysis mostly observes a process as it naturally runs, DOE deliberately changes multiple input factors together in a structured way to efficiently map their effect on the output — far more efficient than testing one factor at a time (OFAT).

Full Factorial 2^k Design

Every combination of k factors at 2 levels each (high/low, coded +1/−1) is run, giving 2^k experimental runs. This estimates every main effect and every interaction effect.

Main effect of A = (ȳ at A⁺) − (ȳ at A⁻)

ANOVA & Regression

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) partitions total variation into between-group and within-group components and compares them via an F-statistic to test whether 3+ group means differ. Regression fits a mathematical model (e.g. Y = b₀ + b₁X) to quantify how a continuous X drives a continuous Y, with R² describing how much of Y's variation the model explains.

F = MSbetween ⁄ MSwithin    R² = 1 − (SSresidual ⁄ SStotal)

FMEA — Failure Mode & Effects Analysis

A structured risk-prioritization tool. For each potential failure mode, the team scores Severity (1–10, impact if it occurs), Occurrence (1–10, how often it happens), and Detection (1–10, how likely current controls catch it before impact — 10 = hardest to detect). Multiplying gives the Risk Priority Number (RPN), used to prioritize corrective action.

RPN = Severity × Occurrence × Detection   (range 1–1000)

Advanced Control & Sustaining Gains

At Black Belt level, control plans extend beyond a single SPC chart to include mistake-proofing (Poka-Yoke), standard operating procedures, response plans for out-of-control signals, and periodic process audits — with statistical ownership formally transferred from the project team to process owners at project closure.
