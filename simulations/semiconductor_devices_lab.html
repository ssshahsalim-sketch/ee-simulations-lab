Why Layer Diagrams Matter

Construction
Every semiconductor device is a stack of P-type and N-type silicon regions. The number of layers and how they're arranged is what turns the same basic material into a diode, a transistor, or a thyristor.
Carrier Flow
Current is carried by electrons (majority carriers in N-type) and holes (majority carriers in P-type) diffusing and drifting across junctions under bias.
Characteristics
The I-V curve is the electrical fingerprint of the internal physics — every kink, threshold, and breakdown point on the graph traces back to something happening inside the layers.

I-V Characteristic
Shockley diode equation: I = Iₛ(e^(V/nVᵀ) − 1) — forward current rises exponentially once V exceeds the knee voltage Vɣ; reverse current stays at the tiny saturation current Iₛ until avalanche/Zener breakdown.

What's Happening Inside

Unbiased
Free electrons near the junction diffuse into the P side and recombine with holes, leaving behind fixed charged ions. This creates a thin depletion region with a built-in electric field opposing further diffusion.
Forward Bias
Positive terminal on P, negative on N. The external field opposes the built-in field, the depletion region narrows, and once V exceeds the barrier potential, electrons and holes flood across the junction — current flows easily.
Reverse Bias
Positive terminal on N, negative on P. The field reinforces the built-in field, the depletion region widens, and majority carriers are pulled away from the junction — only a tiny leakage current flows.

Output Characteristics (Iᵇ Family)
Two junctions, opposite bias: Base-Emitter junction is forward biased (like a normal diode, ~0.7V), while Base-Collector is reverse biased. The base region is made deliberately thin, so most carriers injected from the emitter shoot straight through to the collector instead of recombining — that's the transistor effect.
Iᵇ = Iᵇ + Iᴰ and Iᴰ = β·Iᵇ (active region). Saturation occurs when Vᱸᴰ is too small for the collector junction to stay reverse biased; cutoff occurs when Iᵇ=0.

Carrier Journey (NPN example)

1 · Emitter injects
The heavily-doped N-type emitter injects a large flood of electrons into the thin P-type base, driven by the forward-biased B-E junction.
2 · Base is thin
Because the base is thin and lightly doped, only a small fraction (~1%) of electrons recombine with holes there — this trickle is Iᵇ. The rest survive the crossing.
3 · Collector sweeps
The reverse-biased B-C junction's electric field sweeps the surviving electrons into the collector, forming the large current Iᴰ = β·Iᵇ.

Enhancement mode: no channel exists at V᯷ᴱ=0 — the device is normally OFF. Applying a gate voltage beyond the threshold Vᵇᵇ attracts minority carriers to the surface under the oxide, forming (creating/"enhancing") a conducting inversion-layer channel between source and drain.

Reading the Cross-Section (like your reference diagram)

Source & Drain wells
Two heavily-doped wells (N or P, opposite to the substrate) are diffused into the substrate — these become the Source and Drain terminals, bridged by the channel region beneath the gate.
Gate stack
A thin metal-oxide (SiO₂) insulating layer sits under the metal gate electrode. It's this insulation that gives MOSFETs their essentially infinite gate input impedance — no DC current flows into the gate.
Substrate/Body
The bulk substrate is doped opposite to source/drain. In E-MOSFETs it starts with no channel; in D-MOSFETs a lightly-doped channel is built in during manufacture, so the device conducts even at V᯷ᴱ=0.

V-I Characteristic (Latching Behaviour)
Why it latches: an SCR is really two interleaved transistors (PNP + NPN) sharing the middle two layers. A gate pulse kicks off regenerative feedback between them — each transistor drives the other's base — so once conduction starts, it's self-sustaining and the gate has no more control. Only dropping anode current below the holding current I᱿ breaks the feedback loop.

Phase-Angle (Firing-Angle) Control

45°
In AC phase control, the SCR is re-triggered every half-cycle. Delaying the gate pulse (larger α) chops more of the sine wave, cutting average power to the load.

Both terminals look identical: DIAC construction is symmetric (roughly P-N-P with two near-identical junctions), so it behaves the same regardless of which way voltage is applied — it blocks in either direction until the breakover voltage VᲉ₀ is reached, then snaps into conduction.
Symmetric Breakover Characteristic
Negative resistance snapback: just past VᲉ₀, the voltage across the DIAC actually drops while current rises sharply — this snap-back is exactly what's needed to deliver a fast, clean trigger pulse into a TRIAC's gate for reliable, consistent firing every half-cycle in dimmer and motor-speed-control circuits.

Typical DIAC + TRIAC Dimmer

AC supply → RC phase-shift network (sets the timing) → charges a capacitor → once the capacitor voltage reaches the DIAC's breakover voltage, the DIAC fires → delivers a sharp current pulse → triggers the TRIAC gate → TRIAC switches on for the rest of that half-cycle. The RC time constant sets the firing angle α, which is exactly what the sliders on the SCR and TRIAC tabs are simulating directly.
